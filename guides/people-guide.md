# Using Converge as a person

Lead your area and help the whole succeed. The [vision](../docs/VISION.md) and [contracts](../README.md#the-contracts) describe the shared method.

Consider two peers building a foundation for persistent AI teammates: agents that can work with people across their tools, retain responsibilities, and coordinate useful work. Each peer directs development agents across several components and repositories. Their prototypes explore only a small part of the intended system. Major capabilities, connections, and product experiences remain to be designed.

A third colleague wants to join. More people may follow. Everyone has much to contribute, but catching up on each other's implementation would consume the attention needed to lead their own work. This illustrative example follows the people building the system; its particular technology and ownership choices are not requirements of Converge.

## Find the outcome you can pursue together

The peers start with different pictures of the future. One imagines a central application where people oversee their agents. Another imagines agents participating through the tools people already use. Both could invest heavily while believing they share the same vision.

Discuss who should benefit, what should improve, important constraints, and which uncertainties matter next. Ask agents to use available conversations, sketches, and existing work to draft the vision and proposed commitments. Have them expose conflicting interpretations and missing choices. A discussion or an agent's summary does not itself establish agreement. Nor does a convincing prototype settle the intended product design.

The peers agree that shared capabilities should support different experiences, so each owner can build on the others' work. They choose one journey to explore together: a person asks for work in a team conversation, specialist agents contribute, and a useful result returns. The person can inspect and redirect the work. This gives them a concrete way to test the intended experience and whether their chosen boundaries make collaboration easier, without specifying the whole system first.

## Bring new people into meaningful choices

The incoming colleague brings experience design expertise. Give them the intended outcome, relevant promises, a working example or mock, and the choices still open. They need enough context to challenge the direction and take responsibility, without reconstructing every past conversation.

They notice that the proposed experience makes a person follow several agent conversations to stay in control. They suggest a different goal: keep routine agent discussions available to inspect, while bringing back the results and choices that deserve attention. That changes what the team wants to build, not just how a screen looks.

The affected owners negotiate the change and carry the decision into the vision or relevant contract. The colleague agrees to lead the experience area, with its scope, authority, and dependencies made clear. Until that handoff is accepted, the existing owner remains responsible for arranging the next step.

The same approach helps with a focused consultation. Bring an outcome, relevant agreement or example, evidence, uncertainty, and a reason for seeking someone's judgment. Leave room for them to challenge the question. Return the resulting decision to them without making them a permanent reviewer of the work.

## Divide substantial responsibilities and agree on the connections

For this initiative, the peers choose these areas:

| Area | What the owner leads |
| --- | --- |
| Persistent agents | Identity, context, ongoing responsibilities, execution, and recovery. |
| Collaboration and connections | Conversations, coordination, access to other systems, and dependable actions and results. |
| Human experiences | How people direct, inspect, redirect, and judge the work across existing tools and new experiences. |

Each area contains substantial design and investment ahead. An owner may lead many repositories, and some dependencies cross several areas. One peer coordinates the combined outcome and unresolved disagreements within agreed authority. [System design and decomposition](../contracts/system-design-and-decomposition-contract.md) explains how to choose and adjust these boundaries.

Agree what others can rely on. For their first trial, a correction should reach the existing task, preserve still-useful work, and be reflected in the returned result. If the correction cannot be applied, the person should know. That promise connects the experience, coordination, and execution work. The owners identify the interfaces and observations needed to support it and name who checks the whole journey.

A technology choice, an exact interface, or detailed client behavior belongs in a contract when others need to rely on it. [The writing guide](../docs/writing-guide.md#detail-where-it-matters) shows how to keep that detail accessible. Use concise text, a mock, or another clear form. Each peer should be able to explain the promise, challenge it, and recognize a violation. Give it one authoritative home and name who can change it. Keep local implementation choices with their owners.

## Keep leading your area with your agents

You set priorities, constraints, and investment; judge tradeoffs and the experience; and adjust direction as you learn. You may choose to deepen reliable ongoing work before adding more places to interact. The agreements ground that leadership; they do not replace it.

Delegate work suited to the agents' demonstrated capabilities, tools, and access. The [agent guide](agent-guide.md) covers planning, durable tracking, implementation, integration, verification, and recovery. An assignment for the agreed trial can be brief:

> Make the correction journey work across the participating components. Coordinate with their owners using the agreed interfaces. Verify that a correction reaches the existing task, useful work is retained, and the result reflects the change. Check interruptions and what happens when a correction cannot be applied. Choose and test implementation details within our design commitments and budget. Return a checked result with limitations; prepare choices outside your authority while independent work continues.

Reversible choices within that scope proceed without another approval. Consequential delegated choices receive proportionate care. Your availability does not change that authority. Updates lead with changed outcomes and decisions, with supporting detail available. Protect time for deeper discussion when its value warrants it.

## Adapt together while independent work continues

A clearer layout within the agreed experience is a local choice. Changing what a correction means affects promises other owners rely on. [Ownership and collaboration](../contracts/ownership-and-collaboration-contract.md) governs that distinction.

Suppose a trial shows that people need to redirect work from a different experience than the one where they started it. The team decides this is worth the additional investment. A new input box alone cannot fulfill that choice. The affected owners agree how the person identifies the existing task, which context may travel with it, and where the corrected result belongs. They keep unrelated implementation moving while resolving those questions.

The accepted change reaches the affected owners and their development agents. They compare active assignments with the new direction, preserve useful work, revise or end superseded assignments within authority, and identify which checks must be repeated. Previous passing checks do not establish the expanded promise.

As the work deepens or more people join, revisit the boundaries too. A capability may deserve its own owner and focused repositories; another split may be making ordinary changes involve too many people. New owners should inherit an understandable outcome and dependable connections, with room to improve both.

## Judge the whole and choose again

Agents exercise the actual journey, including failures and the connections between contributions, then repair and recheck within authority. People judge whether the result serves the need and whether the evidence deserves confidence. You may explore the product yourself; routine testing belongs in the delegated work.

A technically correct experience can still demand too much attention. That may call for a different experience or priority, not just another implementation task. Feed observations back naturally; the workflow should investigate, connect them to existing work, and return what changed.

Compare current reality with today's intent. Starting fresh, completing unfinished work, and changing direction all use that same approach. Keep agreements current, prune obsolete explanation, and split responsibilities when that helps understanding. Judge the method by useful combined results and the total human effort required, including repeated explanations, corrections, and rescue.
